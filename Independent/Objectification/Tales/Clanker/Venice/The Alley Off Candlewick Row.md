# The Alley Off Candlewick Row

The tag on Maren's wrist said *VULVA — 25% SURCHARGE APPLIES* in letters so small she had to squint, and it had been saying it since the machine at the bakery door clicked it onto her skin at seven in the morning. By dusk her wrist was chafed from the edge of it, and she was carrying bread, two turnips, and a jar of pickled eggs that had cost her almost a third more than it would have cost her brother.

Her brother. *Was* her brother. Teodor had transitioned at nineteen, four years back, and now he paid full price like any man and the machine at the door beeped him through with a green tag and nobody looked at him twice. Maren had considered it. Most women had, by 1773. Two thirds of them, they said, went through with it before thirty. But Maren liked her body the way it was, or she had, until the world started charging her rent for it.

She was thinking about none of this when it happened.

She was thinking about supper.

---

The alley cut between a cooper's shop and a dye works, and it was the fastest way home from the market, and Maren had walked it a thousand times. In daylight it was nothing. At dusk it was a throat between two buildings, and the only light came from a lamp at the far end that someone had forgotten to trim, so it guttered and wheezed like it was dying.

She heard footsteps behind her. Heavy. Close. Closer than footsteps had any right to be.

She didn't turn around.

That was her first mistake, if you wanted to call it one. Women were taught to keep walking, keep your eyes ahead, don't give them a reason. She kept walking. The footsteps matched her pace. Slowed when she slowed. Sped when she sped.

Then a hand closed around her upper arm and *pulled*, and the bread went one way and the turnips went another and the jar of pickled eggs survived only because it was wedged under her arm like a soldier's last ration.

"Wrong place," the man said. Not to her, exactly. To the alley. To the air. To whatever was listening. "Wrong time."

She knew what he meant. She'd read the reports. Everyone had read the reports, they were printed in the weeklies now like weather. *Female rape on the rise.* *Crimes reported by women ignored by 59% of men.* *Nobody comes when you scream, so don't waste your breath.*

He pushed her against the dye works wall and the brick was still warm from the day's sun, which she found strangely comforting, and then he was on her.

It wasn't gentle and it wasn't violent, it was just *certain*. He knew what he was doing and he did it. He bunched her skirt up past her hips with one hand while the other pinned her shoulder to the brick, and he was inside her before she'd finished understanding that it was happening, and she thought, absurdly, *the pickled eggs*, because the jar was digging into her ribs.

She tried to move her hips to meet him and he grunted like she'd done something clever.

She tried to scream and it came out as a small, breathy noise, because the alley swallowed sound the way it swallowed light. Nobody came. Of course nobody came. There was a whole street on the other side of the wall, twenty feet away, full of people buying ribbons and arguing about the price of coal, and not one of them heard her.

That was the part that got to her later. Not the act. The *silence* of it. The way the world kept turning while a man used her against a wall.

He finished. He pulled out. He adjusted himself and looked at her like she was a shop he'd just stepped out of.

"Sorry," he said. "Didn't mean to be rough."

And then he walked away, out the far end of the alley, past the guttering lamp, and was gone.

Maren stood there with her skirt still up and the wall warm against her back and the jar of pickled eggs pressing into her ribs, and she thought about the fact that he had *apologized*, which somehow made it worse. If he'd been a brute she could have hated him cleanly. But he'd said sorry. He'd said *didn't mean to be rough*, as if there was a correct amount of roughness for a thing like this, as if there was a manual.

She pulled her skirt down.

She picked up the bread. She picked up the turnips. She dusted them off against her thigh because the ground in the alley was wet with something she didn't want to think about.

Then she walked home.

---

Her room was two flights up and the stairs creaked on the fourth step and the seventh, and she knew this because she'd lived there for six years. She put the bread on the board. She put the turnips in the bowl. She set the pickled eggs on the shelf, and she stood in front of the shelf for a long time, looking at the jar, and then she started to cry.

Not because of the rape, exactly. Because of the *jar*. Because she'd carried it through the whole thing. Because she'd held onto it. Because she'd had the presence of mind to hold onto a jar of eggs while a man was inside her, and what did that say about her, that her body was doing one thing and her hands were doing another and her mind was doing a third, and none of them had agreed to it.

She sat down on the edge of the bed. Her wrist tag caught on the blanket and she looked at it. *VULVA — 25% SURCHARGE APPLIES.*

She laughed. It came out wet and ugly.

"Twenty-five percent," she said to the empty room. "And *that's* free."

---

The next morning she went to the constable's office because the weeklies said you should, because *report it, report it*, because if enough women reported it then maybe the 59% of men who ignored them would have to start paying attention.

The constable was a man named Halloran and he had a mustache and a ledger and the tired eyes of someone who had decided long ago that the world was mostly a nuisance.

"Raped," he said, writing it down. "In the alley off Candlewick. Dusk. Man unknown."

"Yes."

"Age?"

"Twenty-six."

"Transitioned?"

"No."

He looked up. "Then you'd have the tax on you anyway. Tag?"

She held up her wrist. He wrote down *VULVA* and didn't laugh, which she appreciated.

"Any witnesses?"

"No."

"Any marks?"

"Bruises on my shoulder. Where he held me."

He wrote that down too. Then he closed the ledger and looked at her with something that might have been sympathy if you squinted.

"We'll look into it," he said.

She walked out and stood on the street and breathed, and she thought about the fact that *we'll look into it* was the same sentence as *nothing will happen*, just dressed up in a collar.

And then she thought: *fine.*

Because she was twenty-six and she had a room and a job at the linen mill and a brother who had transitioned and paid full price and a jar of pickled eggs on her shelf, and she was not going to let one man in one alley in one dying town be the last word on her life.

She went to work.

The tag on her wrist clicked and beeped and charged her the surcharge at the door, and she paid it, and she went in, and she sat down at her loom, and she started to weave.

---

**Epilogue — 1773**

In the weeklies that autumn, a column ran under the heading *"And Still Nobody Came."* It was about the silence of alleys, and the 59%, and the jars of pickled eggs that women carried through things they shouldn't have had to carry anything through.

Maren read it. She didn't write it. But she read it, and she kept the clipping, and she put it in a drawer next to her wrist tag from that day, which she had kept, for no reason she could articulate.

Twenty-five percent.

And *that* was free.
