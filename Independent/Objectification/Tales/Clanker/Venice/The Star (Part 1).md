\#\# Age 8 — The Question

It started with a question, the way the best disasters do.

Katty was eight years old and had just finished her third week of school — third grade, actually, because her parents had homeschooled her into a head start and she'd skipped straight past the littler kids. Third grade meant classmates, and classmates meant the endless, competitive chatter of children discovering the world. And one afternoon the chatter turned to a topic Katty had never considered: \*where do your parents work?\*

Everyone had an answer. A factory. A shop. A farm. A school, like the teacher. And when it was Katty's turn, she opened her mouth and realized she didn't actually know — she knew her dad had lost his job, she knew money was tight, she knew her mom was \*always\* gone and came back smelling strange and happy — but she did not know the \*name\* of the place.

So that night she asked.

Her mom laughed, the tired warm laugh of someone who'd been asked a hundred times before by a hundred grown-ups, and said: "I work at a place where grown-ups go to be \*happy.\*"

"That's not a job."

"It is if you're the one making them happy."

Katty frowned. She was eight and the answer did not satisfy her. So she did what she always did when an answer didn't satisfy her — she \*pushed.\* What do you do there? What makes them happy? Why is it only grown-ups? Can I see it? \*Can I see it?\*

Her mom looked at her dad. Her dad looked at his plate. There was a long pause, the kind that means the grown-ups are deciding something without words.

"Saturday," her mom said finally. "I'll take you Saturday."

\#\# Age 8 — First Visit

Saturday arrived and Katty vibrated the entire way there.

\#\# Age 8 to 12 — The Weekly Apprentice

Every week. Every single week.

She made it her mission. Saturday was \*her\* day at the building. She learned the layout, the rooms, the regulars, the names of the women her mom worked alongside. \#\# Age 14 — The Talk

The day after her birthday, June 9th, her dad sat her down.

He'd been dreading it. He had the speech rehearsed. He was going to explain that money was tight, that school was expensive, that she was old enough and mature enough and \*capable\* enough — and that they'd found her a job, and that the job was where her mother worked, and that she'd start in two days.

He got through about a third of it.

"Yeah," Katty said. "When do I start?"

Her dad blinked. He'd prepared a whole thing. She'd known since she was ten. She'd known \*before\* he did, probably.

"Two days," he said.

"Good." And then she couldn't help it: "Told you so."

Her dad didn't know what he'd been told. Her mom did. Her mom, in the doorway, laughed and laughed.
